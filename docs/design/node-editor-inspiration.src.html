01

Eskimo today: an honest diagnosis

Before borrowing anything, it's worth being precise about what actually looks wrong. Most of it is not styling — it's information density.

  1. The socket matrix is always fully expanded. Every card prints six rows — None / Intro / Transition on the left, None / Outro / Transition on the right — whether or not they carry anything. Across fifty nodes that is three hundred rows of mostly-repeated words. It is the single largest source of visual noise, and no other tool in this study does it.
  2. The word "None" appears twice on every card. Once per side. It labels the absence of a thing. Roughly a hundred instances of the same non-information across the canvas.
  3. Every wire looks identical. Uniform black, uniform weight, no directionality, no type. A transition, a cold cut and an outro handoff are visually indistinguishable — even though the difference between them is the entire point of the app.
  4. Wires route straight through other cards. Long diagonals cross unrelated nodes, so the card boundary stops reading as a boundary. Every tool here solves this with curvature, orthogonal routing, or both.
  5. There is exactly one node size. A four-input hub and a leaf node occupy identical space. Importance is not expressible, and zooming out doesn't simplify anything — it just makes the same text unreadable.
  6. Colour is spent on decoration, and there's no figure/ground. The only colour is the album swatch — which tells you which record the song is from, something the title beside it already said. Meanwhile white cards on light grey with a 1px shadow leave almost no value gap, so the graph reads as a flat sheet of text rather than as objects in space.
Worth saying plainly: the interaction model is fine — toggling sockets, the variant picker, drag-to-connect, autoarrange, multi-select. Several tools in this study do less. This is a presentation problem, not an architecture one.

Where Eskimo sits, and where it could sit

Eskimo is currently at the far left by default rather than by decision — it is restrained, but restraint isn't the same as reliability, and the restraint isn't buying density. The most useful target is around n8n and Bitwig: a light, calm canvas that still uses colour to carry meaning, with one strong idea (signal type) driving the palette.

03

Nuke

Foundry · film compositing · the DAG
Boring but reliable

Nuke's node graph is close to the minimum viable node editor, and it has survived three decades of professional use in exactly that form. Nodes are small coloured bars carrying a name. Flow reads top-to-bottom. There is almost no chrome.

The design system

  • Node colour encodes class — reads, merges, writes and filters each get a family hue, and users can recolour any node to mark up their own script.
  • The node is the label. No subtitle, no metadata, no icon. Size is driven by the name.
  • Postage stamps — optional thumbnails on the node showing what it passes downstream. Critically, they can be set to a static frame or switched off entirely, because on a large script they cost real performance.

Steal this for Eskimo

The postage stamp, and its off switch. A song node showing a waveform with its cue points marked is the direct analogue, and it would make the graph genuinely informative rather than decorative. Nuke's hard-won lesson is that the thumbnail must be optional — Eskimo already renders fifty album swatches with dominant-colour extraction, so the same "turn it off on big graphs" affordance belongs there too.

Colour by class, not by decoration. Nuke spends its entire colour budget on "what kind of node is this". Eskimo currently spends its budget on album art.

Houdini

SideFX · procedural 3D · the network editor
Boring but reliable

Houdini packs more state onto a node than anything else in this study, and does it without the node becoming a mess. The trick is that state is expressed as small, fixed-position marks rather than words.

■ badges — a row of status icons under the name

UX features worth noting

FeatureWhat it solves
FlagsPersistent per-node state (which node is the output, what's bypassed) as always-visible toggles on the node body, not buried in a panel.
BadgesA row of small icons beneath the name carrying status — errors, warnings, comments, time dependency. Glanceable across a whole network.
The node ringHovering a node summons a contextual ring with big click targets for those flags. It exists specifically because the flags themselves become unclickable when zoomed out.
Network boxesLabelled, coloured, resizable containers that group nodes and move them together.

Steal this for Eskimo

The badge row. Eskimo has at least four things it currently either hides or spells out in words: has produced audio, verified splice, local-only vs synced, and contributed-by. Four small marks under the title would replace a lot of text and make the whole library state visible at a glance.

The hover ring is the answer to Eskimo's tiny sockets. The socket dots are ~9px and sit outside the card; at any zoom below 100% they're near-impossible to hit. Houdini hit this exact wall and solved it by escalating the target on hover rather than making the resting state bigger.

05

Blender

Shader, compositor and geometry node editors
Boring but reliable

Blender's node editors are the best mainstream example of a rigorously applied type system expressed as colour. It is not decorative; the colour is the documentation.

The design system

  • One colour per type, everywhere. Learn it once, apply it in every editor.
  • Collapse to a title pill. Any node folds down to just its header — the primary density control, available per node.
  • Sockets are labelled with what they are ("Geometry", "Color"), not with their state.

Steal this for Eskimo

Commit to one socket-type palette and use it on the wires too. Eskimo has exactly four connection types — None, Intro, Outro, Transition. Four colours, applied to the socket dot and the wire, would make the graph self-documenting and directly fix diagnosis #3.

Collapse-to-pill. The cheapest possible fix for the density problem, and it's per-node so the user chooses what matters.

06

TouchDesigner

Derivative · realtime visual systems
Sleek and modern

TouchDesigner's central idea is that a node should show its own output. The node isn't a label for a process, it's a live window onto it. On a dark canvas with restrained chrome, the content becomes the colour.

Steal this for Eskimo

Put the waveform on the card. This is the highest-value idea in the whole study for Eskimo specifically. A song node that shows its waveform with the outro splice point marked turns the graph from a diagram of the set into a picture of the set. The data already exists — rmsEnvelope() is already implemented and already used for the transition preview.

Family colour as wayfinding. Eskimo's equivalent axis is artist, BPM band, or key — any of which would give the canvas structure at a glance.

07

Audulus

Modular synthesis · desktop and iPad
Sleek and modern

The most visually confident tool here, and the closest to "sleek" as an explicit goal. Pure black canvas, hairline neon wires, and a strict two-hue accent palette.

Steal this for Eskimo

Wire craft is most of the perceived polish. Thin beats thick. Curved beats straight. A subtle glow or outer stroke lets wires cross without visually merging. This is a pure CSS/SVG change with no data model implications and would probably move Eskimo's perceived quality more than anything else on this list.

A permanent category palette. Eskimo hides "add a node" behind a right-click. A docked strip would make the vocabulary of the app visible.

08

cables.gl

Browser-native creative coding
Sleek and modern

The most radical density answer in the study, and the most directly applicable to Eskimo's specific problem. cables.gl has many ports per node — and spends almost no space on them.

Steal this for Eskimo

This is the fix for diagnosis #1. Eskimo's six socket rows — roughly 60% of every card's height — could collapse into two edge strips a few pixels tall. The type is the colour; the label appears on hover or on the detail pane. Rectangular ports are also a larger hit target than the current 9px circles despite taking less space, which incidentally helps the drag-connect problem.

09

ComfyUI

Diffusion pipelines · web
Colourful and intuitive

The most-used node editor of the last few years, and a good model for a modern web-native one. Its nodes are forms: they carry real controls, not just wires.

The design system

  • Sockets are named by type, and the name is on the node, right-aligned for outputs and left-aligned for inputs, so the reading order matches the flow.
  • Widgets in the body. Editing the common parameters never requires opening a side panel.
  • Restrained node chrome, saturated wires. The nodes are near-black; all the colour is in the connections.

Steal this for Eskimo

Inline controls. Eskimo's variant picker already lives on the node — that instinct was right. The natural extension is the cue point: a draggable in/out marker directly on the card's waveform, rather than a number in a pane.

Let the wires carry the saturation and keep the cards quiet. This is the opposite of Eskimo's current split, where the cards carry a colour swatch and the wires are black.

10

n8n

Workflow automation · web · light theme
Colourful and intuitive

The single most relevant reference here, because it is the one modern, polished, light-themed node editor in the study — which is the mode Eskimo actually ships in.

UX features worth noting

FeatureWhy it works
Label outside the nodeThe node box stays small and uniform; long names cost nothing because they overflow into empty canvas.
The + on an open outputTurns an empty socket into an invitation. Building a chain never requires finding the node palette.
Dashed vs solid wiresTwo connection classes, distinguished without spending a colour.
Selective output labelsOnly branching nodes label their outputs. Everything else stays clean.
Light canvas, dotted gridProves a light node editor can look modern — it just needs real spacing and a value gap between card and canvas.

Steal this for Eskimo

Move the song title outside the card. Eskimo's cards are sized by their text. Put the title and artist below the card and the card itself can shrink to artwork plus waveform — a large density win that costs nothing.

Add the + affordance to unwired outputs. Eskimo already knows which outputs are open and what could legally connect there.

Label outputs only when they branch. Directly retires the ~100 redundant "None" labels.

Unreal Engine — Blueprints

Epic Games · visual scripting
Colourful and intuitive

Blueprints solve a problem Eskimo has exactly: two different kinds of connection that must never be confused. Unreal's answer is to give them different physical weight, not just different colour.

Thick white execution wires with arrowheads carry order of operations. Thin coloured wires carry data, with the pin colour keyed to type. Title bars are tinted by node category — events crimson, functions blue.

UX features worth noting

  • Execution wires are visually heavier than data wires and carry direction arrows. You can read the control flow of a huge graph at a glance while ignoring the data plumbing.
  • Comment boxes are coloured, titled, and dragging one moves everything inside it — grouping without a formal container type.
  • Wire animation during debugging shows execution travelling along the path in real time.

Steal this for Eskimo

Two weights for two meanings. Eskimo already has this distinction and doesn't draw it: the committed playback path (now frozen in session.committedHop) versus every other possible path. Committed should be heavy with direction; possible should be a hairline. That one change would make "where is this set going" readable from across the room — which for a live DJ tool is the entire job.

The debug wire animation is the model for live playback — and Eskimo already has an animated active-path edge, so this instinct is confirmed by a tool that ships it to millions of users.

12

Bitwig Studio — The Grid

Modular audio inside a DAW
Colourful and intuitive

The closest peer in the entire study: a node editor for audio, built for people who are also DJs and producers, and designed to be legible while something is playing.

Steal this for Eskimo

The whole signal-type colour model transfers directly. Eskimo's four connection types are its signal types. Bitwig demonstrates that in an audio tool this reads as professional rather than childish, provided the palette is disciplined — saturated wires, desaturated everything else.

Inline controls read as an instrument. Bitwig's nodes look like gear. Eskimo's look like a spreadsheet row. A waveform, a cue marker and a small level readout would close most of that gap.

What they all agree on

Individual tricks are interesting; unanimity is actionable. Five patterns appear in essentially every tool studied — and Eskimo does none of them.

PatternWho does itEskimo today
Connections are colour-coded by type Blender, ComfyUI, Bitwig, Unreal, cables.gl, TouchDesigner All wires identical black
A per-node density control Blender (collapse), Nuke (stamps off), Houdini (badges scale), ComfyUI (chevron) None — always maximum detail
The node displays its own content TouchDesigner, Nuke, Substance, ComfyUI, Bitwig Album art only — decorative, not informative
Labels only where they carry information n8n, cables.gl, Nuke, Houdini Six labels per card regardless of state
Node size or shape varies with role Blender, Unreal, ComfyUI, n8n, Bitwig One fixed card size for everything

The one genuine disagreement

Canvas value. Eight of the ten use a dark canvas; n8n is the notable exception and it looks excellent. So light is a legitimate choice — but it is the harder one, because a light canvas gives you a much smaller value range to separate card from background. n8n buys that separation with generous whitespace, a crisp card border radius, and by keeping nodes small. Eskimo currently pays for a light canvas without buying any of the three.

The short version

Eskimo doesn't look unprofessional because it's under-designed. It looks unprofessional because it spends its space on labels and its colour on decoration, when every tool that reads as professional does the reverse: space goes to content, colour goes to meaning.

Recommendations for Eskimo

Ordered by impact per unit of work. The first four are the ones that would change how the app feels; the rest are refinement.

  1. Collapse the socket matrix into edge strips. cables.gl · Two thin coloured strips instead of six labelled rows. Recovers roughly 60% of card height, removes ~300 rows of text from the canvas, and gives a bigger hit target than the current circles. Highest impact by a wide margin.
  2. Colour wires by connection type; two weights for committed vs possible. Bitwig · Unreal · Blender · Four hues for None / Intro / Outro / Transition. Heavy stroke with direction for the committed path, hairline for everything else. Pure rendering change — no data model work, since committedHop already exists.
  3. Put the waveform on the card. TouchDesigner · Nuke · With the splice point marked. The envelope data and the renderer already exist for the transition preview. Make it disable-able on large graphs, as Nuke does.
  4. Move title and artist outside the card. n8n · Lets the card shrink to art plus waveform and stops long titles from driving layout.
  5. Thin the wires and improve routing. Audulus · 1–1.5px with a soft outer stroke so crossings stay readable, and enough curvature that wires stop cutting through unrelated cards.
  6. Add a badge row. Houdini · Has audio, verified splice, local-only, contributed — four marks replacing four different text treatments.
  7. Collapse-to-pill per node. Blender · The user's own density control, and the natural home for a zoom-based level-of-detail rule later.
  8. A + affordance on unwired outputs. n8n · Turns an empty socket into the primary way to build a set.
  9. Escalate socket targets on hover. Houdini's node ring · Fixes small-target hit testing at low zoom without enlarging the resting state.
  10. A permanent category strip for adding nodes. Audulus · Makes the app's vocabulary visible instead of hiding it behind a right-click.
Sequencing note. Items 1–4 are mutually reinforcing and should be designed together — shrinking the card, moving the label out, and adding the waveform are really one redesign of the node, not four. Item 2 is independent and could ship on its own tomorrow.

What this does not solve

Edge strips lose the ability to label a specific target song on the card ("Transition → I Am a God"). That information has to move somewhere — hover, the detail pane, or a label on the wire itself. Wire labels are what Blender and Unreal would do, but they add their own clutter at scale. This is a real trade-off and worth deciding deliberately rather than by default.

The current build already has the hard parts: a committed-hop model, an envelope renderer, dominant-colour extraction, dark theme tokens, and a working drag-connect. Every recommendation here is presentation sitting on top of those.
